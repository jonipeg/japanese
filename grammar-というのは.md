# というのは {#というのは}
#japanese-grammar
#grammar

**Kanji form:** というのは

**Furigana form:** というのは

**Meaning:** "The reason is that..., what I mean is..." — a conjunction that introduces an explanation or elaboration clarifying/justifying the statement just made.

**Conjugation:** Sentence-initial conjunction, no conjugation needed — attaches at the sentence boundary, connecting the previous sentence to the explanatory sentence that follows (often itself ending in 〜からだ/〜のだ, similar to なぜなら, though this ending is optional rather than obligatory). It can also appear mid-sentence after a noun or quoted term (「残業代」というのは〜 "what is meant by 'overtime pay' is...") to introduce a definition or clarification of that term, a distinct but related usage from its sentence-connecting role.

**Typical context:** Written and spoken, neutral-to-formal register — natural in both explanatory writing (essays, articles) and careful spoken explanation (lectures, conversations where someone clarifies their meaning). It is less stiff than なぜなら and more versatile, fitting comfortably into semi-casual conversation when someone is explaining themselves, while still working in formal written contexts.

**Similar grammar structures:**
- [**なぜなら**](grammar-なぜなら.md) — formal written; similarly introduces a reason for the preceding statement, but なぜなら is more rigid/formal and requires a からだ/のだ ending, whereas というのは is more flexible and usable in speech.
- [**だから**](grammar-だから.md) — casual, spoken; states a forward-looking result/conclusion rather than looking back to explain a reason.
- [**それで**](grammar-それで.md) — casual-neutral, spoken; narrates a result rather than explaining/clarifying a prior statement.
- [**だって**](grammar-だって.md) — very casual, spoken; a whiny, excuse-making equivalent, lacking というのは's calm, explanatory tone.
- [**そのため**](grammar-そのため.md) — neutral-formal written; states a result that follows from a cause, rather than explaining the reason behind a claim.

**Opposite grammar structures:** No direct opposite; its structural counterpart is a conclusion/result conjunction like したがって or そのため, which moves forward from cause to effect, whereas というのは moves backward from claim to explanation.

**Example sentences:**
1. 今日は休ませてもらいます。というのは、昨夜からずっと熱があるんです。
   (I'll be taking the day off today. The reason is, I've had a fever since last night.)
2. 彼の提案には賛成できない。というのは、実現可能性がほとんどないからだ。
   (I can't agree with his proposal. The reason is that it's almost impossible to realize.)
3. 「ワークライフバランス」というのは、仕事と私生活の調和を意味する言葉だ。
   (What is meant by "work-life balance" is a term referring to the harmony between work and personal life.)
4. このレストランはいつも混んでいる。というのは、味がいい上に値段も手頃だからだ。
   (This restaurant is always crowded. The reason is that the food is good and the prices are reasonable too.)
5. 彼女は留学を諦めた。というのは、家庭の事情で長期間家を離れられなくなったのだ。
   (She gave up on studying abroad. The reason was that family circumstances made it impossible for her to leave home for a long period.)

**Common mistakes / key nuances:** Learners sometimes treat というのは as a stricter, more formal construction like なぜなら and feel obligated to end the sentence with からだ — in fact というのは is more flexible and can end a sentence more naturally without it, which can make it feel more approachable/conversational in contexts where なぜなら would sound too formal. The other common confusion is mixing up というのは's "explaining/clarifying a term or statement" function with its "introducing a reason" function — both exist, and getting the intended meaning from context (is a word being defined, or is a prior claim being justified?) is key to using and understanding it correctly. Using だって in place of というのは in a composed explanation (e.g. to a boss) is also a common register mistake, since だって sounds like a childish excuse rather than a calm clarification.
