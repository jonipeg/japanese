# ～てやる {#てやる}
#japanese-grammar
#grammar

**Kanji form:** ～てやる

**Furigana form:** ～てやる *(no kanji; やる is conventionally written in kana as the auxiliary here)*

**Meaning:** Benefactive auxiliary meaning "do (something) for someone" from a position of equal or higher status than the recipient; can also express a confrontational/determined "I'll show you/do X to you" nuance, often used to express spite, determination, or defiance.

**Conjugation:**
- 動詞て形 (verb て-form) + やる
- Plain, casual conjugation; politer benefactive equivalents are ～てあげる (neutral/polite) and ～てさしあげる (very polite, for superiors) — てやる is the casual/rough register, used toward subordinates, animals, plants, or in defiant statements about oneself.

**Typical context:** Casual spoken Japanese; can sound rough, blunt, or even aggressive depending on context. When used in its "benefactive for someone lower in status" sense (e.g., watering a plant, helping a younger sibling), it's casual but not rude. When used in its confrontational sense ("I'll show them!"), it carries a defiant, sometimes angry or determined emotional charge — inappropriate in formal or polite contexts.

**Similar grammar structures:**
- **てあげる** — the standard, neutral-to-polite benefactive ("do something for someone"), usable toward equals or people slightly below you without the rough/confrontational edge that てやる can carry.
- **てさしあげる** — the humble/polite benefactive used toward social superiors; てやる would be considered rude or inappropriate toward a superior, where てさしあげる is required.
- **てくれる** — "someone does something for me/my in-group"; describes the direction of benefit toward the speaker, whereas てやる describes the speaker (or someone in their in-group) doing something for someone else, often lower in status.

**Opposite grammar structures:** The directional opposite is ～てもらう / ～てくれる ("to receive the favor of someone doing something for me"), which reverses the direction of benefit from "I do for you" (てやる) to "you do for me" (てもらう/てくれる). No single "opposite" in the confrontational sense, though a resigned/defeated statement like ～てしまう could contrast with the defiant determination of confrontational てやる.

**Example sentences:**
1. こんな会社、いつでも辞めてやるわ！
   (A company like this — I'll quit it anytime I please!)
2. みんなにお前の秘密をばらしてやるぞ。
   (I'll tell everyone your secret, just you watch!)
3. 弟の宿題を手伝ってやった。
   (I helped my little brother with his homework.)
4. 庭の花に毎朝水をやっている。
   (I water the flowers in the garden every morning.) *(note: 水をやる itself is a base benefactive-like usage, directly giving to a plant/animal)*
5. 絶対に見返してやる、覚えてろよ。
   (I'll definitely show them up someday — just you wait.)

**Common mistakes / key nuances:** Learners often use てやる toward a social superior out of a mistaken belief that it's simply the casual version of てあげる — this is a common mistake, since てやる can sound presumptuous or rude when the recipient outranks the speaker (てさしあげる or simply avoiding the benefactive construction is safer in those contexts). Another key nuance: the same てやる form can mean either a kind favor (toward someone/something clearly subordinate, like a pet or younger sibling) or an aggressive threat/vow (toward a rival or unfair situation) — the emotional tone must be inferred entirely from context, as the grammar itself doesn't disambiguate.
