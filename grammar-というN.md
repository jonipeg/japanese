# ～というN {#というN}
#japanese-grammar
#grammar

**Kanji form:** ～というN *(repetition pattern, no additional kanji)*

**Furigana form:** ～というN

**Meaning:** "Every single N / N after N (emphatic totality)" — repeats the same noun on both sides of という to emphasize that without exception, all instances of that noun are affected.

**Conjugation:**
- 名詞 + という + 同じ名詞 (N というN)
- Example base: ビル → ビルというビル

**Typical context:** Formal, written/literary register; used for dramatic emphasis in descriptive or narrative writing, especially to describe widespread destruction, change, or occurrence. Not used in casual conversation.

**Similar grammar structures:**
- **あらゆるN** — "every kind of N"; a more neutral, everyday way to express totality/comprehensiveness, lacking というN's dramatic, almost literary repetition emphasis.
- **すべてのN** — plain "all N"; the most neutral totality expression, usable anywhere, without the vivid emphatic flavor of というN's doubling.
- **というものは** — a related but distinct construction meaning "as for the general nature of N" (used to make generalizations about a category), not an emphatic repetition of total scope.

**Opposite grammar structures:** 一部のN（だけ）("only some N") expresses the reverse — a partial rather than total scope.

**Example sentences:**
1. 激しい地震で街中のビルというビルが崩れ落ちた。 (In the violent earthquake, every single building throughout the town collapsed.)
2. その台風で木という木がなぎ倒された。 (In that typhoon, every single tree was knocked down.)
3. 戦火によって家という家が焼け落ちた。 (Due to the fires of war, every single house burned down.)
4. 彼の演説を聞こうと、駅前は人という人で埋め尽くされた。 (Wanting to hear his speech, the area in front of the station was packed with every kind of person imaginable.)
5. その噂は瞬く間に町中の店という店に広まった。 (That rumor spread in an instant to every single shop throughout the town.)

**Common mistakes / key nuances:** というN requires repeating the exact same noun on both sides — it cannot be used with two different nouns, which is a common error for learners trying to force general "all kinds of X" meaning onto it (for that, あらゆるN is correct instead). This pattern is almost always used for dramatic, sweeping statements (destruction, crowding, spreading), so it sounds odd in mundane, low-stakes contexts.
