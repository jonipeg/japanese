# ～をものともせず(に) {#をものともせず}
#japanese-grammar
#grammar
#to-review

**Kanji form:** ～をものともせず(に)

**Furigana form:** ～をものともせず(に)

**Meaning:** "Undaunted by ~" / "Without being discouraged by ~" — describes someone pushing forward energetically despite an obstacle or hardship that would normally make a person give up or feel defeated.

**Conjugation:**
- 名詞 ＋ をものともせず(に)
- 彼は体の障害をものともせずに、精力的に活動している — "undaunted by his physical disability, he is energetically active"

**Typical context:** Written, formal/literary register — common in news stories, biographies, speeches, and admiring descriptions of a third party's courage or perseverance (e.g. athletes, disaster survivors, activists).

**Similar grammar structures:**
- [**～にもかかわらず**](grammar-にかかわらず・にもかかわらず.md) — "despite ~," a more neutral/general concessive that can describe any situation, not necessarily one requiring courage; lacks the admiring, "overcame adversity" nuance.
- [**～をよそに**](grammar-をよそに.md) — "ignoring ~ / paying no heed to ~," similarly describes disregarding something, but often carries a critical nuance (ignoring others' worries or warnings) rather than praise.
- **～物ともせず** is itself sometimes written with the kanji 物, but the meaning and usage are identical.

**Opposite grammar structures:** No direct grammatical opposite; a phrase like ～に負けて ("giving in to ~") or ～に屈して ("succumbing to ~") would express the reverse — being defeated by the obstacle rather than overcoming it.

**Example sentences:**
1. 彼は体の障害をものともせずに、精力的に活動している。
   (Undaunted by his physical disability, he is energetically active.)
2. 母は強かった。がんの宣告をものともせず、最期まで明るくふるまった。
   (My mother was strong. Undaunted by her cancer diagnosis, she stayed cheerful until the very end.)
3. 隊員たちは危険をものともせずに、行方不明の人の捜索を続けた。
   (The rescue team continued searching for the missing person, undaunted by the danger.)
4. 彼女は周囲の反対をものともせず、自分の夢を追い続けた。
   (Undaunted by opposition from those around her, she kept pursuing her dream.)
5. その選手は度重なるけがをものともせず、現役を続けている。
   (Undaunted by repeated injuries, the athlete continues to compete.)

**Common mistakes / key nuances:** This pattern attaches only to nouns describing a serious obstacle or hardship (typhoon, illness, danger, opposition, injury, etc.) and the sentence as a whole praises the bravery or resilience of the subject — it is not used for trivial inconveniences. It is almost always used about a third party, not the speaker's own actions, since praising oneself this way would sound unnaturally boastful.
