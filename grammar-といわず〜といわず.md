# ～といわず〜といわず {#といわず〜といわず}
#japanese-grammar
#grammar

**Kanji form:** ～と言わず～と言わず

**Furigana form:** ～と言(い)わず～と言(い)わず

**Meaning:** "Whether it's X or Y" / "both X and Y alike" — indicates that a described state or action applies equally and indiscriminately across two (often representative, body-part or location) examples, implying it actually applies everywhere.

**Conjugation:**
- 名詞A + といわず、名詞B + といわず
- Requires exactly two parallel nouns (often contrasting parts of a whole, e.g., body parts, times, places) in a fixed "AといわずBといわず" frame, followed by a predicate describing the uniformly-applying state.

**Typical context:** Neutral-to-formal written/spoken register; frequently used descriptively (e.g., describing an injury, a mess, or weather conditions affecting everything uniformly). Not rude; a vivid, somewhat literary way of emphasizing totality/indiscriminateness.

**Similar grammar structures:**
- [**なり〜なり**](grammar-なり〜なり.md) — means "either X or Y (whichever)," used for presenting alternative options/choices to select from; unlike といわず〜といわず, it does NOT mean "both apply equally" — it offers a disjunctive choice.
- **というか〜というか** — means "so to speak, X or maybe Y," used when the speaker is unsure how best to characterize something, hedging between two descriptive terms; different because it expresses speaker uncertainty about characterization, not uniform applicability across examples.
- **つ〜つ** — means "alternately doing X and Y" (e.g., 行きつ戻りつ "going back and forth"); describes alternating actions, not simultaneous uniform applicability.

**Opposite grammar structures:** No single direct opposite, though a phrase like ～はもちろん～は別として ("X is of course one thing, but Y is treated separately") would conceptually invert the "equally applies to both" sense into "these are treated differently." No direct opposite.

**Example sentences:**
1. けんかした子供は手といわず足といわず、引っかき傷だらけだった。
   (The child who'd been in a fight was covered in scratches — hands, feet, everywhere alike.)
2. 台風の後、庭の木といわず屋根といわず、至る所に被害が出ていた。
   (After the typhoon, damage appeared everywhere — the garden trees, the roof, all alike.)
3. 彼の部屋は机の上といわず床といわず、本が散乱していた。
   (His room had books scattered everywhere — the desk, the floor, all alike.)
4. その絵画展は壁といわず天井といわず、作品で埋め尽くされていた。
   (That art exhibition had every surface covered with works — walls, ceiling, all alike.)
5. 引っ越し作業で、顔といわず服といわず、ほこりだらけになってしまった。
   (During the move, I ended up covered in dust everywhere — face, clothes, all alike.)

**Common mistakes / key nuances:** Confused on graded tests with なり〜なり, というか〜というか, and つ〜つ. The crucial distinguishing feature: といわず〜といわず requires two specific, often body-part-like or spatial examples standing in for "everywhere/every part," with a predicate that uniformly and often negatively applies to all of them (e.g., damage, injury, mess) — it is NOT presenting a choice (なり〜なり) or hedged description (というか〜というか), and it does NOT describe alternating action (つ〜つ). Students should check whether the sentence implies totality/indiscrimination rather than choice or alternation.
